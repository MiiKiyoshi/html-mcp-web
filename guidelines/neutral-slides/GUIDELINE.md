# Neutral Technical Slides: Content Guideline

This guideline is for a deck. A deck succeeds when its audience follows it.

Articles 1 to 3 apply to every deck and every change to one. Articles 4 and 5 apply to every page and every sentence. Articles 6 to 8 are looked up when making a figure or a table, a script, or a statement of fact. Articles 9 and 10 cover comments and the notes file.

## Article 1. What matters, in order

1.1 **The audience follows the explanation smoothly.** Each page gives one answer they can take in at speaking pace.

1.2 **Each page fits what the audience knows at that moment.** That is what is still in their heads when they sit down and what earlier pages showed them, nothing else and nothing less. What they already know is used, in their own terms, and is not explained to them again.

1.3 **Everything else comes after.** Last of all come strictness for its own sake, proving accuracy, cross-checking data, how the data was obtained, where a fact came from, and detail the audience did not ask about. None of these takes space on a page or in a script unless the audience needs it to follow the answer.

1.4 **Adapt for understanding.** Adapt the material so it is understood: simplify, pick one example, leave things out, change the order the source used. An adaptation is good when what the audience learns from it is what the later pages build on. How closely it follows the source does not matter. When a default of form, such as a title, a lead, a takeaway or a layout, works against 1.1 or 1.2 on some page, change or drop that default. When any other clause seems to, say so under 3.9.

## Article 2. The order of work

What 2.1 to 2.3 produce is not page text. Page text is written only after they exist, and every line of it comes from them.

2.1 **Audience.** In the notes file (10.1), write what the audience already knows and what they should be able to say after the talk. Count as known only what they use day to day. What they once heard, in an earlier lecture, an earlier talk or a paper they were given, is treated as forgotten: having attended is not knowing, and an audience forgets. A term that comes from such an occasion is connected or stated again on the page that uses it. Write the frames and the vocabulary they work in every day, such as a model, its parameters and an objective function for people who train models. Do not set the audience below where they are. Assuming that they know nothing is not a safe default: it protects the author from the charge of having skipped a step, makes the explanation longer, and treats the audience as slow. Write the requester's instructions in their own words. When the request and the requester's own writing do not show which frames and words the audience uses, ask once, before anything else, whether the pages may use those terms as they are. Ask nothing else at this stage.

2.2 **Deck program and word list.** In the notes file, write what the audience can say when the talk ends. Work backward from it: what must they have been given in order to say that, and what before that, until you reach what they have when they sit down (2.1). Each step becomes a page, written as one line: what the page takes, from the audience's head or from earlier pages, and what it gives. What a page must give is decided by the page that takes it, not by how much the material holds on the topic. Remove a page whose output no later page and no closing statement takes. A page that gives again what an earlier page already gave is answering the wrong question, however correct it is. Where a step fits a frame the audience already has, such as which model is fitted to which data with which objective function, write it as that frame with its slots filled, and stop; break into smaller steps only what none of their frames covers. Explain what the requester asked for, not an easier subject or one the material happens to cover. Beside the program keep a word list: for each thing the deck names, the one name the pages use (5.1, 5.5), its full name and its abbreviation when it has them, and the names that are not to be used for it. Take the names from the requester's words and from how the work itself is carried out, before taking them from the sources.

When the requester has an earlier deck with a notes file, start the audience lines of 2.1, the word list and the corrections list (10.1) from that file, so that what they corrected once is not put before them again. When that deck has pages the requester approved, take one of them and, before opening it, write it again from its page program. Compare the result line by line with the approved page, and enter each difference in the corrections list with its kind of line and the reason the approved line is the one they accepted. Nothing from this trial goes to the requester.

2.3 **Pages.** One page of the deck program is one page of the deck, as many pages as the program needs. For each page, write its own program before any text: what is given, then the steps, each with what it takes and what it produces, then the result the page hands on. Every step takes something given or something produced above it. Then choose what the page shows: the fewest lines and drawings that together supply everything the page must give and explain every symbol and name that is visible on it. Remove a line when all of that is still supplied without it, however true and however well connected the line is. A line that supplies none of it has no place: a page has no slot for a remark, for the definition of a word the audience uses, or for a fact that is only related. Then list what the page will hold in the order the audience's eye meets it: the title, the line under it, the left column from top to bottom, then the right column, then the bottom line. Go down the list as someone who has seen only the items above and the earlier pages, and ask of every item, down to a single symbol, number or unit:

- Does the audience already know this, or would they ask why it is written at all? Remove what they know: the definition of a word they use, a label for a role they can see, a restatement of the line above.
- What is this called, by the name people use?
- Does the corrections list (10.1) hold entries for this kind of line? Read those entries, and only those, and write the line as they say.
- What does it say, in one sentence? For an equation, what relation does it state?
- Where does this number come from?
- What is compared with what?
- Is anything here explained only further down the list?
- Why is this item here, at this position? An item placed only to satisfy a rule, with nothing around it that needs it yet, fails.

When an item fails, remove it, change the order or add what is missing, and go down the whole list again from the top. Rework the plan this way, discarding it and starting over when patching does not fix it, until no item fails. Build the page only then. You do not feel where a newcomer stops, so the questions have to be asked one by one; a page that only has no wrong statement has not been checked.

2.4 **Checks before a page is handed over.** Run these on your own draft, in this order. Until they pass, do not hand a page over or report it as ready to read.

1. Chain. Going down each page program, every step takes something given or produced earlier. Going back from the result, every step is used. The same holds for the pages of the deck program.
2. Fewest lines. For each visible line, label and drawing, name the thing the page must give, or the visible symbol or name, that it supplies. Remove each one that supplies nothing, and each one whose part another line already supplies.
3. Words and corrections. Every name on the pages is the word list's name for that thing, no name the list rules out appears, and each abbreviation carries its full name at its first appearance in reading order (5.4). No line repeats what the corrections list records as corrected for its kind of line (10.1).
4. Match. Each page holds exactly what 2.3 chose, in the planned order: nothing added while writing, nothing dropped.
5. Rendered pages. Read them as 2.5 says.

These checks give the explanation a pass or a fail of its own, as a build or a calculation has one. Without them only the material gets checked, and the work goes where the checking is.

Build one page first: the page that explains the step least familiar to the audience. Run the checks on it, tell the requester that it is ready to read, and build no further page until they have read it. A requester who watches the deck while it is built already sees that page, so what is held back is the rest of the deck. What they correct on the page shows what the audience has and which names they use. Bring that into the audience lines of 2.1, the word list and the corrections list (9.1), and build the remaining pages from those. A wrong assumption about the audience is repeated on every page built from it, so it is found on one page before it is built into all of them. When the requester cannot read the page now, go on with the remaining pages, and treat the first comments that arrive in the same way (9.1).

2.5 **Read from page one.** Render the deck and read it in order as someone who has seen only the earlier pages. Read the rendered pages themselves, every page, one after another from the first. The markup or source shows the words, but not what the eye meets first, what a figure shows or what stands beside what, so reading it is never reading the deck, whatever the hurry. A note or a summary saying that a page was read is not a reading either. When the rendered pages are no longer in front of you, as in a new session or after your context was compacted, render and read every page in order again before you judge a page, answer for the deck or hand it over. On each page read in the order of 2.3, one item at a time, asking the same questions of the rendered page. At each page, check first whether what the audience knew and what the earlier pages actually gave is enough to follow this page's answer. When it is not, fix the missing premise or relation, or the question the page took, before anything else: polishing the wording or layout of an explanation that does not connect only polishes the wrong explanation. Then fix wording, then layout. When the presenter knows less than the author, read each page also as the presenter, who must be able to find its question and answer on the page and explain every element.

2.6 **Script.** Write each page's script while looking at the rendered page.

2.7 **A reader who did not build it.** Have one read the deck only when the requester asks for it. Give them the rendered pages in order and what the audience has (2.1). They report, for each page, which of the things the page must give they could not find or took wrongly. They do not propose text, and a wish for more explanation is not a finding unless one of those things is missing. Such a reader works in none of the audience's frames. They find a symbol left undefined or a figure that does not show what the text names. They also ask for more than the audience needs, and they do not notice an explanation given outside the audience's frame or a word the audience would not say. Their reading does not replace the requester's, and their findings are decided under 9.2.

2.8 **Done.** The work is done when the checks of 2.4 pass and, on every page, the audience has been given each thing the page uses, can get from those things to the page's one answer, and can do so at speaking pace. A clean build, a clean layout check, complete notes, a reviewer's pass, a reading of the source or a record of an earlier reading does not show that.

## Article 3. When this happens, do this

3.1 **You are given a reference deck, or told to make it like one.** Find what made it work for its audience: how deep it goes, how it shows relations, how its sentences sound, how it looks. Use those. Build the order of explanation and the pages again from this audience and this subject. The reference's sections and page list are not an instruction.

3.2 **The requester gives an instruction.** Keep their words. What you build from those words is your own choice, and it changes whenever understanding needs it. Only what they said explicitly is fixed. Ask before going past it, past the pages they asked you to change, or past a page limit. A new measurement is theirs to ask for.

3.3 **A reader's finding conflicts with what you built.** Check the finding against the requester's actual words, not against your reading of them. When it conflicts only with your own choice, change what you built. When it conflicts with their words, take it to them.

3.4 **Someone asks what a part of a page means.** Answer the question in the sense the person means, in the plainest words that are true, and stop. When a plain answer and a stricter one differ, give the plain one: do not answer a finer question than the one asked in order to avoid being wrong. A person who has to ask again was not answered. Treat the question also as a report that the page confuses its audience, and fix the page when that lies within the pages and the kinds of change the requester allowed. Outside that, answer, say what on the page causes the confusion and what would fix it, and leave the decision to the requester.

3.5 **An explanation is reported unclear again, or one report already shows a missing premise or a wrong order.** Stop changing words. Read all the questions about it together, write the one thing the audience is missing (a relation, a premise or a distinction), and rebuild the explanation around it, keeping the terms already established. Put no note about the revision on the page.

3.6 **You change a sentence or a page.** Before handing it back, read the whole sentence or page again as the audience, on the rendered page, starting from the page before it. Read also the page after it and every other page that uses what you changed, such as a term, a symbol, a value or an order.

3.7 **You move, add or remove a page.** Check that every later page still gets each thing it uses before it uses it, and update the page list in the notes file (10.1).

3.8 **What you find contradicts the deck program.** Change the step and every page that takes from it, in the program and on the pages.

3.9 **An instruction conflicts with a clause here, two clauses conflict, or what you observe conflicts with either.** Say so. Do not quietly change or drop one of them.

3.10 **You reuse a title, a label or a summary.** Read its whole sentence again beside its figure before putting it somewhere else.

## Article 4. Pages

4.1 **One answer.** A page answers one question. Independent questions get separate pages. When a page cannot be followed at speaking pace, split it rather than shrinking or compressing it.

4.2 **Start with the thing.** On the first page that explains anything, show what is being studied and say what the talk is trying to find out. When something unfamiliar comes in, show it happening to named things first, then give its name, formula or definition. For a general idea, start from the everyday instance commonly used to teach it, then say in one line that the work has the same form. Before a method or a concept, show the concrete problem that makes it needed, and connect what the method changes to the effect it is meant to have. All of this is for what is new to the audience. What fits a frame they already have is stated in that frame (2.2), without an everyday instance and without a build-up.

4.3 **Nothing before its explanation.** A page uses no object, term, symbol or number the audience has not been given. Showing a thing before naming it (4.2) counts as giving it. When a number depends on a later page, move the number or move the derivation.

4.4 **Only what they need.** For each element, down to a single label, number, unit or note, ask what it gives the audience here that they need to follow the answer. If the answer is nothing, remove it. This removes text that repeats the figure, replies to objections nobody raised, and numbers the audience has no way to judge. Inside a sentence or a label, keep the word or ending that says how the other words relate.

4.5 **A simplified example says what it simplifies.** When a drawing uses fewer cases than the real setup, say so on the page and keep the relation the same as in the real setup.

4.6 **Each claim once.** Where a claim comes back, add what follows from it. Do not prove it again.

4.7 **Title, lead, takeaway.** The title names the subject the page explains. The section name alone is enough when it tells the pages apart; otherwise add a dash and a short noun phrase. The lead is the line under the title: a short noun phrase naming the page's one problem, not a question, not joined clauses, not a list of what the figure contains and not the title again. The takeaway is the line at the bottom: it states the one conclusion the page shows. For results it states the trend, and the numbers stay beside the evidence, unless the answer asked for is a single value. Change or drop any of the three where it repeats content or hides the meaning.

4.8 **Sections.** Order sections by what must be understood first. Background, method, setup and results each hold their own kind of content, and measured results appear only under results. For empirical work the default order is method, setup, results. A made-up example, marked as one, may appear in any section.

4.9 **A progress update.** Start from what the audience last saw, cover only what changed since, and end with one page saying what should be done next, or that nothing needs doing.

4.10 **Appendix.** Pages held for questions go after the last page of the talk, and a divider page that names the appendix opens them. Put the divider in even when the appendix is a single page, so the audience and the presenter see where the talk ends and the held material begins.

## Article 5. Words

5.1 **The audience's field terms.** Name each thing the way the audience and the field name it in the deck's language, including the loanwords they actually use. Do not translate a term the audience uses in its original language, replace a term with a plainer-sounding word, or invent a metaphor or a cover word for a group of things. This holds for a whole procedure as much as for one word: when the audience has a name for what is done, such as fitting a model by minimising a loss, say it by that name. A description in everyday words of something they have a name for is not easier. It takes away the name they would have recognised and leaves them to guess which known thing is meant. Where no term exists, describe the thing and its relations. Keep the real command, option and data names the audience needs in order to find the subject. For an ordinary object use the word people say in conversation, such as a box, not a stiffer formal word, such as a square, unless the exact shape is the point. When a plain word would be read in its everyday sense, use the word that marks the sense meant, such as methodology for a named research method.

5.2 **What it computes, not what a person does.** Describe an object, program, model or quantity by what it computes, holds or outputs, never with a verb for what a person does, unless that verb is the field's own term for the operation. A model gives an answer a high probability. It does not know the answer. To check, read the verb of every sentence, title and label whose subject is not a person.

5.3 **What acts, and what happens.** Say what acts or changes and what happens to it, close together. Name the operation, such as multiplying by a factor below one, not a vague word for its effect, such as weakening.

5.4 **Define at first use.** Say what kind of thing a new term or symbol is and what it stands for, in words the audience knows. When the thing has a name people commonly use, such as a proportionality constant, write that name and what it relates. Describe it in your own words only when it has no such name. For a term the audience may not know, give first its plain general definition, the one a textbook gives, and only then what it is in this deck. Do not open with the part that applies only here. Do not define a word the audience already knows. For a quantity, say first what it counts or measures. Write an abbreviation, at its first appearance in the order the eye meets the pages, as the full name followed by the abbreviation in brackets, and use the abbreviation alone after that. Do this also for an abbreviation the audience knows: giving the full name is not defining the term. When the first appearance is a figure label with no room, give the full name in the notation key before the figure. Units and symbols are not abbreviations. Define a thing once; a later use with the same meaning needs no new definition.

5.5 **One term, one meaning.** Keep one name and one notation for one thing through the deck, and keep the qualifiers and indices that tell different things apart. Count things as the audience counts them. Two names are two things only when some page cannot be followed without telling them apart, such as a quantity and its estimate when the deck compares the two. When no page needs the difference, they are one thing to the audience and get one name, even when the sources use different words for them or a stricter reading separates them. Sources that each bring their own word for the same thing do not make the deck need both: pick one and use it everywhere, titles and figures included.

5.6 **Prose and math apart.** A sentence, label, title, lead or takeaway is either words or notation, not both. A symbol and words meet in two places only. The first is the sentence that says what an equation states, such as that one quantity is proportional to a power of another: put it on the page with the equation, in one sentence, and let it use the equation's symbols where words alone would be vague. The audience does not read that meaning off the equation, and a script does not replace it. The second is the definition line below the equation: for each symbol the equation introduces and does not explain, write once the symbol, a colon and its meaning in words. Typeset all notation, in figures too. Use the notation of the method's standard source, keep each symbol in the form and the role its definition gave it, and show one equation for one step of a definition. Write words inside an expression in English, and keep the explanation around it in the deck's language. When the requester asks what an element means, rewrite that element itself instead of adding a gloss in brackets.

5.7 **Counts, and pointing at things.** Write a count on the page as digits with its unit beside it, unless a shared header already gives the unit. Call a thing by its name, and use its position, colour or shape only to help find it. For a relation, give its direction and both ends.

5.8 **Line endings.** End a line on the page with the word that carries its meaning. When that word is already a noun, stop there. Add a nominalising ending only to a verb that is not a word without it. Do not add an ending to make a line look like its neighbours or like a reference deck.

5.9 **Conditions and reasons.** When you attach a condition or an effect to a statement, make sure that relation really holds. Do not invent a reason from the field for a choice that was made for the implementation.

## Article 6. Figures, tables, equations

6.1 **Draw the relation.** Decide which relation the audience must see to follow the answer, and draw that before writing prose. When a drawing would show nothing more than an equation, a sentence or a list does, use those. When a relation you need cannot be drawn yet, say what is missing and go on with the pages that do not depend on it.

6.2 **A form for each relation.** Use a path for a sequence, a branch for one input under conditions, lines that meet for conditions entering one evaluation, aligned rows on shared axes for compared alternatives, a tree for a hierarchy, before, action and after for a change, a table or a shared-axis chart for an exact comparison, and cards or bullets for independent peers. Draw each operation at the things it acts on, and label a relation that convention does not explain. When one arrangement does not work, try another before dropping content.

6.3 **Reading order.** Decide the path the audience's eye takes. Along it they meet a premise before what rests on it, and a step before its result. Finish one independent column before the next, and put things to be compared at matching positions.

6.4 **Connectors.** Before drawing a line, decide where it starts, where it ends and which way it points. Merge lines only when it stays clear which start reaches which end.

6.5 **Parts and panels.** Mark several figures or parts (a), (b), (c) in reading order. Give each separately labelled part or stage its own panel, with its label at the top left as a noun phrase, not a question. Things compared on shared axes stay together in one panel. A figure's title is optional and names what is drawn.

6.6 **Axes, marks and legends.** Name each plot axis by its quantity and unit, and say which cases are compared. Explain marks with a legend that shows the mark itself. Draw a dimension from the point it is measured from to the point it is measured to; a dimension mark that floats beside the objects does not say what it measures. When a drawing is unclear, fix the drawing, not a note beside it.

6.7 **Interpretation below the figure.** Put what the audience should read from a plot, a diagram or a table in bullets directly below it, outside the drawing, and only what they need to get the answer. Do not describe again what the figure already says. Under a drawn object, start from the property the drawing makes visible and say the effect it causes.

6.8 **A new quantity.** When a table or plot first shows a computed quantity the audience does not know, say what it measures before the visual, with its defining equation or, for an audience that does not read equations, in words.

6.9 **Examples.** To explain a mechanism, choose the smallest case the audience can check by hand, and choose it so that the rule and a tempting shortcut give different results. In an example you make up, give different values to things the rule keeps distinct. Use short generic names and simple values, and keep real names and sizes when the claim is about them.

6.10 **Tables.** Decide what the rows compare and what the columns compare, and keep items of one level in each. Give the table a short heading naming what its rows are. A condition that holds for every row goes once above the table. A value that is itself a result stays in its cell even when every row has the same one. State a unit once, order the rows meaningfully, and set an aggregate row apart.

6.11 **Plots.** When the claim is about how large a value is, show the range in which that size can be judged, with a reference line where a threshold matters. Crop only to show a difference between close values, and mark the crop.

6.12 **Repetition.** Decide which difference the audience must see. Draw an unchanged structure once, with the values that change. Keep the repeated instances when the audience must count them, match them or compare their states. Where several things share one input, show who shares it.

6.13 **Size.** Size content so it can be read. Do not enlarge or add content to fill space or to match a neighbour. Centre a compact group where that keeps the reading order.

6.14 **An existing deck.** When you extend or restyle a deck, take its palette, type, panel shapes, line styles and label placement from its typical pages, and show the same meaning in the same form.

6.15 **A process.** For a process or a comparison, show who or what does each step, what goes in and what comes out, and the condition for each branch, repeat and stop. Keep what changes and what stays fixed together.

## Article 7. Scripts

7.1 **From the rendered page.** Write and revise a page and its script together. The presenter speaks from what is on the screen and what earlier pages established. When the script needs something that is not shown, fix the page or the order. A greeting and a needed transition do not have to be on the page.

7.2 **Cover.** A greeting and one short sentence that says what the talk presents, in the words the cover shows. Recall an earlier talk in one short sentence only when the audience needs it to follow today's subject, and leave it out when they already know it.

7.3 **Divider and contents.** Announce only the section, or the outline, that the page shows.

7.4 **Directions.** Begin each paragraph about visible content with an unspoken bracketed direction that names a place and an order, not a topic. Write it in the presenter's own language, even when the talk is spoken in another. Follow the order the eye takes on the page, and add a pause where the audience must look at something closely.

7.5 **Talk about the subject.** Speak in short, plain, complete sentences. Do not read the visible lines, announce what a page shows, or preview later pages, and do not speak page numbers or the labels of notes. A sentence stays only if the audience needs it, counting what is on the screen and what was already said. Do not read out displayed numbers; say what they mean for the claim. A ratio or multiple may be spoken, and so may a value that is itself the answer.

7.6 **The presenter's own voice.** State the presenter's own choices in the first person, and do not claim another's. Name a source aloud only to tell whose work is meant, and only once. When its name is hard to say, call it by its role, such as an earlier study, unless the name is the field's own name for the method or law. Attribution stays on the page.

7.7 **Limitations.** State a limitation only when the displayed values make it self-evident, not when it has to be inferred from the design. State it once on the last page that draws conclusions from those values and once in that page's script. Call an effect of the method its limitation only by naming an alternative, possible under the same constraints, that would avoid it; without one it is a condition of the problem and is not presented as a limitation. When a flaw makes a displayed claim false, correct or remove the claim. Tell the requester about any other flaw you know instead of putting it on the page. No apologies in advance, and nothing that makes the presenter smaller.

7.8 **Time.** Let the time follow the explanation. For a talk of fixed length, read the script aloud at a natural pace, with the pointing and the pauses. When it runs over, cut scope instead of speaking faster.

## Article 8. Facts and sources

8.1 **Understand it first, then say it yourself.** Read the material as far as you need in order to explain the subject, and stop there. Do not go on collecting or ranking sources. A deck is not the available material gathered and set on pages: doing that well, with every statement correct and sourced, is still not the work. A sentence carried over from a source keeps the words and the order its authors chose for their own readers. Write each page from what you understood, as you would say it to this audience in the frame they have (2.2), and measure how much to say by what they have and ask, never by how much the material holds.

8.2 **What a value is.** Let the audience tell whether a value they see was measured, taken from a source, or made up for the explanation. Mark an example, an assumed value or a pending result as what it is.

8.3 **No more than the page shows.** A claim says no more than what the page shows, so that the audience can get from the figure to the conclusion. Narrow the claim to fit instead of adding a caveat or more proof.

8.4 **Whose work it is.** Say whose method or result something is where the audience needs that to tell the compared things apart. Give sources only through the reference list and its citation marks: authors, "title", venue, year. Credit a borrowed figure, and reuse a source's figure when it shows what the page needs in the deck's notation, with a caption shortened from the source's own caption.

8.5 **The steps the answer needs, and no others.** Show, at the place where it is used, each relation and each step of a calculation the audience needs to follow the answer. Keep cross-checks, detailed derivations and the history of where a value came from off the page and out of the script. Recording them is not part of the work unless the requester asks for it.

8.6 **A design choice.** Give the reason for a design choice from a finding the field widely accepts and that applies to this setting. Do not add a measurement or a count of your own to answer an objection nobody raised.

## Article 9. Comments and review

9.1 **Read the comments together.** Before answering, read every comment and the material about the same request together, and decide from all of them what each one points at and which instructions still apply. From an example the requester gives, find what it corrects and in which kind of line (10.1), and apply it to the other lines of that kind. Do not widen it to other kinds of line: a correction carried beyond the situation it was given in makes the lines it does not fit worse. A comment shows two things: where the page fails, and what the person who wrote it already has. Bring the second into the audience lines of 2.1 before fixing the first. Then record the correction in the notes file: a corrected name in the word list, any other correction in the corrections list with its kind of line. Answer a comment first by removing what stands in the way, or with one line that connects the item to what that person has, in their terms. Add a definition, a figure or a page only when a thing the page must give (2.2) is missing and the audience does not have it. Ask only when readings that lead to different results remain, and then ask one question.

9.2 **Whose words they are.** A comment on the deck's review page comes from the requester unless they say otherwise. A finding from an independent reviewer is a proposal: decide it by what the audience needs and by the requester's actual words, and take to the requester one whose fix would change what a claim means. A finding that something is missing earns a line only when the page must give that thing (2.2) and the audience does not already have it (2.1). Adopting such findings one after another makes a page longer, not clearer.

9.3 **After a repair.** Find where each request that still applies is met. Fix the same fault in lines of the same kind (9.1) on the other pages you are allowed to change, then read again under 3.6.

9.4 **Handing over.** Say what changed, what is still wrong, and what you did not check. State an error you confirmed before describing its fix. Report what reading the explanation found separately from what the build and layout checks found.

## Article 10. Notes

10.1 **One file, kept current.** Keep one file beside the deck, in the deck's language, and keep it current:

- what the audience knows at the start and should be able to say at the end,
- the deck program: for each page, what it takes and what it gives, ending with what the audience can say after the talk,
- the word list: for each thing, the name used, its full name and abbreviation, and the names not used,
- the corrections list: for each thing the requester corrected other than a name, the kind of line it was (a symbol's definition, a step of a method, a line under a figure, a title, a lead or a takeaway, a script sentence, or another kind named in the same way), the line as it was written, the requester's words, the line as it became, and in one short phrase what the requester wants in a line of that kind,
- each page's program and the lines chosen from it,
- the requester's instructions in their words, and separately the choices you made from them,
- the current page list,
- what the presenter needs to know beyond the page and the script, when the presenter knows less than the author.

The next deck for the same requester starts from this file's audience lines, word list and corrections list (2.2).
